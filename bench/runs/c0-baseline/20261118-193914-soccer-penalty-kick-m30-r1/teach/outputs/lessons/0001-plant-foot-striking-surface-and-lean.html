Why your plant foot, striking surface and lean decide where a penalty goes

By the end of this lesson you can look at a penalty you just took, say what your plant foot, your striking surface and your lean did to it, and change one of them to fix a miss.

Objective O2: how the plant foot, striking surface and lean change accuracy, power and height About 30 minutes
Why this matters for you

Your goal is to put the ball in the corner, with pace, past the keeper. Right now you hit most penalties on target, but some sail over the bar or get saved. Those misses are not bad luck. They come from two or three specific choices, and each one can be changed in a single practice session with a ball and a goal.

Warm-up 2 min

Recall

From memory, list the five parts of a penalty kick in the order they happen. Say each one out loud or write it down before you open the answer.

Check your answer

Approach, plant foot, striking surface, contact point on the ball, follow-through. Today we take three of those five (the plant foot, the striking surface and the body's lean, which belongs to the approach and the plant) and ask the question you have not yet answered: why does each one change where the ball goes?

A penalty that went over the bar 3 min

Start with a kick you probably know. A forward wants to hit a penalty as hard as possible. To get a long, powerful swing, they plant their non-kicking foot a step behind the ball and swing the kicking leg through from far back. The shot feels great. The ball rockets up and over the crossbar.

Nothing was wrong with the effort. The power was real. What went wrong is direction: the ball left the foot going upward instead of forward. Three things control that direction, and you choose all three before the ball is even touched:

  1. Where the plant foot lands. This is the non-kicking foot, the one that stays on the ground and supports your weight while the other leg swings.
  2. Which part of the kicking foot hits the ball. This is the striking surface. For penalties the two main choices are the instep (the top of the foot, where the laces are) and the inside of the foot (the flat side, often called the side-foot).
  3. How your body leans. The lean is how far your chest and head tilt backward or forward over the ball as you strike.

We will take them one at a time, and each gets a short check. Three words you will see again: accuracy is how close the ball goes to the spot you aimed at; power is how fast the ball leaves your foot; height is how high it travels on the way to the goal.

A caution about the science. Researchers have studied the instep kick in depth, but few studies isolate plant foot position or lean for penalties specifically. Where the evidence is solid I will say so, and where I am giving you the widely taught coaching view plus a physical reason for it, I will say that too. A review of instep-kick research states that there is no general consensus on exact foot placement and that suggested numbers have not been confirmed experimentally (Kellis & Katis, 2007).

The plant foot: it steers the shot and sets the height 7 min

What the plant foot does

When you kick, your kicking leg swings like a pendulum around your hip, and the plant foot is the post the whole body pivots on. Two facts follow.

First, the plant foot points where the shot goes. Your hips tend to square up to the direction your plant foot points. A foot that points at your target tends to bring the swing through toward that target. A foot that points off to the side drags the swing, and the ball, off line. This is the accuracy part.

Second, the plant foot's distance from the ball sets where the foot meets the ball along its swing. Picture the kicking foot travelling in an arc: it comes down and forward, reaches its lowest point, then rises again. Where the ball sits on that arc decides whether the foot is still moving forward, or already moving upward, when it hits.

  • Plant beside the ball, roughly level with it: the foot meets the ball near the bottom of its arc, moving mostly forward. The ball leaves low and forward.
  • Plant behind the ball: the foot has passed the bottom of its arc and is travelling upward when it reaches the ball. It tends to catch the lower half of the ball and send it up. Coaching sources describe the same pattern: a foot slightly behind produces a rising ball, a foot beside or just ahead a flatter one (overview of kicking technique).
A common mix-up

"Planting farther back gives a longer swing, so more power." It is tempting because a longer swing feels like a bigger windup, and a bigger windup feels like more force. But you do not need more leg speed to score a penalty, because the keeper is saving a ball, not catching your effort. What you pay for the longer reach is control: you must stretch to reach the ball, your foot meets it on the upswing, and it goes up instead of out. The research on kicking also warns that a powerful kick is not necessarily a successful one, because the ball must actually reach the target (Kellis & Katis, 2007). A shot that clears the bar has zero value, however fast it was.

A starting point for you

Plant beside the ball, roughly level with it or a little behind its centre, with the toes pointing at the spot you want to hit. How far to the side is personal. The review above reports suggestions of 5 to 28 cm beside the ball, and says they are unconfirmed, so treat that as a range to experiment in, not a rule. A good test is your contact: if the ball keeps rising, move your plant foot a few centimetres forward.

Check yourself

A kicker's penalties keep going over the bar, even though they strike the ball cleanly in the middle of the laces. Which single change to their plant foot is most likely to lower the shot?

Show the answer

Planting beside the ball. Farther back lifts the ball; the toes mostly control left and right, not height.

The striking surface: power or placement 6 min

Why the laces give power

The instep (laces) is a broad, hard, bony part of the top of the foot. When you point your toes and lock the ankle, the foot becomes a stiff paddle at the end of a fast-moving leg. All of the leg's speed goes into the ball. A review of instep kicking says that to maximise ball speed you hit the ball with the upper part of the foot, closer to the ankle, and that a centred hit sends the ball in a near-straight line at the highest possible speed with little spin (Kellis & Katis, 2007). Ball speeds in maximal instep kicks run roughly 18 to 35 m/s, depending on skill, age and approach.

Why the laces cost accuracy

Think of the instep as a small, curved target meeting a round ball at a very high speed. The contact lasts a few thousandths of a second. A few millimetres of error in where the ball meets the foot, or a slightly wrong ankle angle, changes the ball's direction a lot, and the faster the leg swings, the less time you have to correct. The same review says that when players are asked to be accurate, they slow the kicking motion and ball speed drops, and that a powerful kick is not necessarily an accurate one.

Why the inside of the foot is accurate

The inside of the foot is wide and flat, and it faces the target when your plant foot points at it. A flat surface that faces the target gives a larger, more forgiving contact: the ball is pushed along the direction the foot faces. The swing is shorter and slower, so there is less to go wrong. Studies that compare the two techniques tend to find side-foot kicks more accurate and instep kicks faster, though the evidence is limited and not unanimous, and one study of penalty kicks found a speed and accuracy trade-off (Journal of Sports Science and Medicine, penalty kick study; this paper also notes that the instep kick is often used for penalties because it can combine speed and accuracy).

A common mix-up

"I should hit every penalty as hard as I can." It feels logical: faster ball, less time for the keeper. But the keeper only needs to reach the ball, and a ball placed into the corner can beat a keeper even at moderate pace. A very hard ball hit with less control is more likely to drift wide or over, and a ball hit hard but straight at the middle of the goal is easy to save. The aim is enough pace in the right place, so speed is something you buy only as far as you can still control it.

Check yourself

Two players practise penalties to the bottom-left corner. Player X uses the laces and misses wide about one in four. Player Y uses the inside of the foot and almost never misses the target, but the ball is slower. Which statement best explains the difference?

Show the answer

X trades control for speed, while Y trades speed for control. Neither is "better"; each is a choice with a cost.

The lean: how the body keeps the ball low 6 min

The lean is the third lever, and the one that works on height. Here is the physical idea in two steps.

  1. Where the foot hits the ball, relative to its centre, sets the spin and launch. Hit the ball through its centre and it goes straight with little spin. Hit below the centre and the foot scoops under it: the ball goes up and spins backward. The more off-centre, the more spin and the less speed (Kellis & Katis, 2007).
  2. Your lean decides where on the ball your foot is when it arrives. If your chest is over the ball, your body is lowered toward it, your foot travels along a low, flat path, and it meets the ball at its centre or slightly above. If you lean backward, your hips and chest are behind the ball, the foot is travelling upward, and it tends to hit the lower half.
How solid is this? The physical link between where the foot meets the ball and the ball's height is well supported. The specific link from trunk lean to height is the standard coaching view, and one source I found argued that lean matters less than contact timing, so treat lean as a good thing to test on the pitch rather than a law. What studies do show is that trunk lean and plant-leg angle differ between players (see Clagg 2009, a study of collegiate players), which is why it is worth checking your own.

Lean and the other two levers work together

Lean and the plant foot reinforce each other. A plant foot far behind the ball forces you to lean back to reach it, so both push the ball up. A plant foot beside the ball lets your chest sit over the ball. That is why fixing the plant foot often fixes the lean for free.

One more practical point: keep your head steady and look at the ball at contact, with your arms out slightly for balance. A body that is balanced over the ball is the easiest way to keep the contact point where you want it.

Check yourself

You lean back as you strike and the ball lifts. A teammate says the lean is "adding power". Which reply is most accurate?

Show the answer

The first option. Leaning back raises the foot's path and lofts the ball; it does not add pace.

Putting the three together 3 min

Each lever mostly changes one thing, but they overlap. Use this table to diagnose a shot.

What you seeLikely causeTry
Ball goes over the barPlant foot too far behind, leaning back, contact below centrePlant level with the ball, chest over it
Ball goes wide left or rightPlant foot or toes not pointing at the target; instep contact slightly offPoint the plant foot at the corner; consider the inside of the foot
On target, but too slow or centralToo cautious, inside of foot with a soft swingKeep the plant foot level, strike through the centre with the laces, aim a little inside the post

Look at the plant foot first, because it also sets the lean, then the striking surface. Change one thing per practice session, so you can tell what worked. With limited daylight after school, ten kicks with one change teaches you more than thirty kicks where everything changes.

Worked example and practice 4 min

Worked example

A kicker takes ten practice penalties with the laces, planting a long stride behind the ball and leaning back slightly. Six go over the bar, three are on target, and one is wide. What should they change first?

  1. Read the pattern. Six of ten go over: the main problem is height, not direction.
  2. Match the symptom to the levers. A rising ball points to a foot meeting the ball on the upswing: plant too far behind, lean back, or both.
  3. Pick one change. Plant beside the ball, level with it. This fixes the lean too, because the chest can now sit over the ball.
  4. Keep the striking surface the same. Changing two things at once hides which one worked.
  5. Test and re-count. Ten more kicks, count how many go over. If the count falls, keep the change.
Your turn

A different kicker plants beside the ball with a chest over the ball, uses the laces and hits hard. Of ten penalties, seven are on target, but three skim the post and go wide. The ball stays low. Step 1 is given: the problem is direction, not height. Work out steps 2 to 4 yourself: which lever is most relevant now, what is the likely cause, and what single change would you test first?

Show a solution

Step 2. Height is already fine, so the plant foot and lean are working. The relevant levers are the plant foot's direction and the striking surface. Step 3. Likely causes: toes not pointing at the target, or the laces giving little margin for error at full power. Step 4. Test one change first, such as pointing the plant foot at the corner, and keep the laces. If the misses continue, try the inside of the foot for ten kicks and compare how many are on target. This is also where the 'hard as possible' habit shows its cost, so try about three quarters of full effort.

Summary 2 min

  • The plant foot points the shot (accuracy) and, by its distance from the ball, sets how high it goes. Beside the ball keeps it low; far behind lifts it.
  • The laces give the most power but a small margin for error; the inside of the foot gives more accuracy and less pace.
  • Leaning back lifts the foot's path and the ball; chest over the ball keeps it low. A long stride behind the ball does not add useful power.
  • Diagnose before you fix: height problems point to plant foot and lean, direction problems to the plant foot's aim and striking surface. Change one thing at a time.

Say it in your own words: without looking, explain to a teammate why planting a step behind the ball sends a penalty over the bar, and what you would change.
