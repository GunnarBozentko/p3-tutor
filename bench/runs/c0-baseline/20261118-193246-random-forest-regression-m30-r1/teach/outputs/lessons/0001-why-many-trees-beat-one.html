Why averaging many trees beats one deep tree

By the end of this lesson you can explain, and compute, why the average of many regression trees has a lower error than a single deep tree, and say what the average can and cannot fix.

Objective O2: how adding multiple decision trees improves regression performance About 30 minutes
Why this matters for you

Random forest regression is exactly this idea: many trees, averaged. If you can explain why the average beats one deep tree, you can answer the bias-variance questions an exam will ask about forests, and you will know what to tune when you apply one to a large dataset.

Warm-up 2 min

Recall

From memory: when a new point lands in a leaf of a regression tree, what number does the tree predict? And what does a tree grown until every leaf has one training row do on new data?

Check your answer

The tree predicts the mean of the training targets in that leaf. A tree grown to single-row leaves memorises the training data, including its noise, so it predicts new data poorly: low bias, high variance. Today is about the second half of that sentence: how to keep the low bias and cut the variance.

The problem: one deep tree is jumpy 4 min

Start with an everyday example. You want to know how long your commute takes. You time it on one random morning and get 41 minutes. That morning had a stalled bus, so 41 is a poor guess for a typical day. The measurement was honest, but one sample is at the mercy of that day's luck.

A deep regression tree is like that one morning. It fits the particular training rows it saw, noise included. Train it on a different sample of rows from the same source and the splits, and so the predictions, change a lot. This sensitivity to the training sample is called variance: how much the prediction at a fixed input would move if you retrained on fresh data. You already know that a deep tree has low bias and high variance; the scikit-learn guide and most textbooks describe the same trade.

A simple engineering parallel: a sensor reading has noise. You do not fix noise by building a more complicated sensor; you take many readings and average them. The noise in each reading is random, so the errors partly cancel.

Check yourself

You train the same deep tree on two different random samples of 500 houses. The two trees disagree on the price of one particular house by $60,000. Which property of deep trees does this show?

Show the answer

High variance. The prediction depends heavily on which sample the tree was trained on.

The fix: average many trees 5 min

Do what you do with the commute: collect many predictions and average them. In a random forest regressor you grow many trees, each on a different bootstrap sample, and the prediction for a new point is the average of the trees' predictions (Wikipedia: Random forest). A bootstrap sample is a sample of the same size as the training set, drawn from it at random with replacement, so some rows appear twice and some not at all. Building trees on bootstrap samples and averaging them is called bagging (bootstrap aggregating); the scikit-learn documentation describes it as building several instances of an estimator on random subsets of the training set and combining their predictions.

Two facts make this work, and you should be able to state both:

  1. Each deep tree is nearly unbiased but noisy. Its errors are partly random, pointing up for one tree and down for another.
  2. Averaging cancels random errors but keeps what the trees agree on. If all trees see the real trend, the average keeps it. The noise that differs between trees shrinks.

So the average has about the same bias as one tree and lower variance. Lower variance with the same bias means lower expected squared error. That is the whole answer to your question about why many trees beat one deep tree: you are not making any tree smarter, you are averaging out the part of each tree's error that is luck.

Why the steps differ: bootstrap samples contain different rows, so the best split threshold lands in a different place each time. Section 4 covers how to make trees differ even more.
Check yourself

A forest of 200 deep trees predicts better than any one of its trees. Which statement best explains why?

Show the answer

Random errors partly cancel, so variance falls while bias stays about the same.

The math: variance of an average 6 min

Let each tree's prediction at a fixed input be a random variable (random because the training sample is random) with variance σ². Take the average of B such trees. If the trees were completely independent, the variance of the average would be σ²/B: 100 trees, 100 times less variance.

But trees trained on overlapping data from the same source are not independent. Suppose each pair has correlation ρ, a number from 0 (unrelated errors) to 1 (identical errors). Then the standard result for the average of B identically distributed variables is given in the random forest literature and in Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, chapter 15:

Var(average) = ρ·σ²  +  (1 − ρ)·σ² / B

Read it as two terms. The second term, (1 − ρ)σ²/B, shrinks as you add trees. The first, ρσ², does not depend on B at all. It is the shared part of the error: whatever all the trees get wrong together, averaging cannot remove.

Two limits show the idea. If ρ = 1 (every tree identical) the formula gives σ² for any B: a hundred copies of the same tree are no better than one. If ρ = 0 it gives σ²/B. Real forests sit between: the trick is to push ρ down.

A common mix-up

"Enough trees drives the error to zero." It is tempting because the independent case gives σ²/B. But trees share training data, so ρ > 0 and the variance stops at ρσ². This is why forests improve quickly for the first tens of trees and then flatten. Adding more trees is cheap and does not hurt, but past the flat part it does not help either. To go lower you must lower ρ, which is the next section.

Check yourself

Trees have variance σ² = 8 and pairwise correlation ρ = 0.5. What is the lowest variance the average can reach, however many trees you add?

Show the answer

The floor is ρσ² = 0.5 × 8 = 4. The variance gets arbitrarily close to 4 as B grows but never goes below it.

Making trees different 5 min

The formula says the lever is ρ. Bagging already lowers it by giving each tree a different bootstrap sample. A random forest goes one step further: at every split, the tree may consider only a random subset of the features, not all of them. This is the standard difference between bagging and a random forest, as the Wikipedia article explains.

Why that helps: suppose one feature is by far the strongest. Without the restriction, almost every tree splits on it first, so all the trees look alike (high ρ). With the restriction, many splits cannot use the strong feature, so trees must find other good splits, and the trees become less alike. Each individual tree gets a bit worse (slightly higher bias), but the average improves because the floor ρσ² drops. In scikit-learn this is the max_features setting of RandomForestRegressor.

Check yourself

You raise max_features so each split sees all features, instead of a small random subset. What happens to the trees in the forest?

Show the answer

The trees become more alike, so ρ rises and the variance floor ρσ² rises with it.

What averaging cannot do 3 min

Two limits, one of which you met in your first session.

A common mix-up

"The average of many trees will continue a rising trend past the data." It will not. Every tree predicts a mean of training targets, so the average of trees is also an average of training targets. It can never leave the range of the training targets. Figure 1 shows it: the thick line gets finer steps, but beyond the last split it is flat, like each tree. The forest is smoother between training points, not a line that continues past them. If your data trend beyond the training range, a forest will underpredict.

Second, averaging attacks variance, not bias. If every tree is systematically wrong in the same way, for example because the trees are too shallow to capture the pattern, the average inherits that error. That is why forests use deep trees: you want low-bias trees, and you pay for their variance with averaging.

Worked example and practice 5 min

Worked example

A single deep tree has prediction variance σ² = 4 at some input. In a forest, pairs of trees have correlation ρ = 0.5. Find the variance of the average for B = 10 trees, and compare with B = 100.

  1. Write the formula: Var = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B. Reason: trees are correlated, so the simple σ²/B is wrong.
  2. Shared term: ρσ² = 0.5 × 4 = 2. This does not depend on B.
  3. B = 10: (1 − 0.5) × 4 / 10 = 0.2, so Var = 2 + 0.2 = 2.2.
  4. B = 100: 0.5 × 4 / 100 = 0.02, so Var = 2 + 0.02 = 2.02.
  5. Interpret: going from 10 to 100 trees cut the variance by only 0.18, from 2.2 to 2.02. The floor of 2 dominates. Lowering ρ would help far more than adding trees.
Your turn

Trees have σ² = 10 and ρ = 0.3. First step: write down the shared term ρσ². Then find the variance of the average for B = 10, and say what the variance floor is.

Show a solution

Shared term: 0.3 × 10 = 3. Second term for B = 10: (1 − 0.3) × 10 / 10 = 0.7. Variance = 3 + 0.7 = 3.7. The floor, as B grows without limit, is 3. One tree would have had variance 10, so 10 trees already remove most of the avoidable part.

Explain it

A classmate says: "My forest underpredicts houses bigger than anything in the training data. I'll add 5,000 more trees to fix it." In two sentences, say whether that will work and why.

Show a solution

It will not work. Every tree predicts a mean of training prices, so the average cannot go beyond the training range, and more trees only reduce variance, which is not the source of this error.

Summary 2 min

  • A deep tree has low bias and high variance. Averaging many trees keeps the bias about the same and lowers the variance.
  • Variance of the average: ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B. Adding trees shrinks the second term only; the floor ρσ² remains.
  • Lowering the correlation ρ, by bootstrap samples and random features at each split, is what makes the average pay off.
  • The average cannot extrapolate beyond the training targets and cannot fix bias shared by all the trees.

Say it in your own words: without looking, explain why the average of 100 deep trees beats one deep tree, and what stops the improvement from continuing forever.
