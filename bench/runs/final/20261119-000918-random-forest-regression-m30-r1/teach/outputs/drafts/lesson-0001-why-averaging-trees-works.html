Why averaging many trees beats one deep tree

By the end of this lesson you can explain, with a formula and a number, why the average of many decision trees predicts new data better than any single deep tree.

Warm-up 2 min

Two ideas are needed here: how a tree predicts, and what the variance of a mean looks like.

Recall 1

In one sentence each, what do the bias and the variance of a predictor mean? Which does a fully grown tree have more of?

Check your answer

Bias is the average gap between the prediction and the truth over possible training sets. Variance is how much the prediction moves when the training set changes. A fully grown tree has low bias and high variance.

Recall 2

Two independent predictions each have variance 100. What is the variance of their mean?

Check your answer

(1/4)(100 + 100) = 50. Averaging independent errors halves the variance. The rest of the lesson asks what changes when the predictions are not independent.

Recall 3

A quantity X has variance 100. What is the variance of X/2?

Check your answer

(1/2)² × 100 = 25. This is the same 1/B² step the derivation below uses.

The idea: many noisy scouts 5 min

A deep tree fits its training data almost perfectly, so it has low bias, meaning its predictions are right on average over possible training sets. It also has high variance, meaning its predictions change a lot when the training set changes a little. Averaging attacks the variance directly: if many predictors make different mistakes, the mistakes partly cancel in the average. The ensemble is the group of trees, and a random forest is an ensemble of deep trees whose regression output is the average of the trees' predictions (Wikipedia: Random forest).

Consider a soccer club valuing one player. A single scout watches three matches, and a lucky hat-trick inflates the estimate; ten scouts watching different matches disagree, but their average is steadier than any one report. Different matches play the role of different bootstrap samples, which are random resamples of the training rows, defined in the next section. Ten scouts copying the same notes add nothing: that is a correlation of 1, where correlation measures how alike two reports are, from 0 (unrelated) to 1 (identical). The more the reports overlap, the smaller the gain from each added scout.

Averaging also smooths the prediction. Tree 1 jumps from 200 to 260 at 1,800 square feet, and tree 2 jumps from 200 to 260 at 2,000. Their average is 200 below 1,800, 230 between 1,800 and 2,000, and 260 above 2,000. With 100 trees at different split points there are many small steps, close to a smooth curve. Outside the training range every tree is flat, so the forest is still flat there.

Five trees predict a house whose true price is 250 (thousands of dollars):

  • Predictions: 262, 238, 255, 271, 244.
  • Errors: +12, −12, +5, +21, −6.
  • Squared errors: 144, 144, 25, 441, 36; their mean is 158.
  • Average prediction: (262 + 238 + 255 + 271 + 244) / 5 = 254, error +4, squared error 16.
  • Averaging cut the squared error from 158 to 16, about a factor of ten.
Check yourself

Why does averaging five trees give a smaller error than the typical single tree in the example above?

Show the answer

The middle option is right. The five errors are +12, −12, +5, +21 and −6, and their mean is +4. Cancellation shrinks the error; it does not remove it.

How the trees are made different 5 min

Training the same algorithm on the same data five times gives five identical trees, so there is nothing to average. A random forest therefore injects randomness in two places. Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample: n rows drawn with replacement from the n training rows, so some rows repeat and about a third are left out. At each split, the tree considers only a random subset of the features (scikit-learn user guide: ensembles).

A small example with five rows, A to E:

  • One bootstrap draw is B, B, D, A, E: row B appears twice and row C is left out.
  • One draw misses a given row with probability 1 − 1/5 = 0.8, and all five draws miss it with probability 0.85 = 0.328.
  • A row is therefore in the sample with probability 0.672, and the expected number of distinct rows is 5 × 0.672 = 3.36.
  • For larger n the left-out share (1 − 1/n)n is 0.349 for n = 10 and 0.366 for n = 100, and approaches 1/e = 0.368.

The second source of randomness, the feature subset, matters because of one strong feature. Without it, every tree would split on that feature first, and all trees would look alike. Restricting the candidates forces trees to find different splits, which makes their errors less alike. Note that the original forest algorithm uses a subset for every split, while the scikit-learn regressor by default considers all features. That default is plain bagging (bootstrap aggregating): each tree trains on its own bootstrap sample and the trees are averaged (RandomForestRegressor documentation).

Check yourself

A dataset has 10 features and each split considers 3 of them, chosen at random. Why does this lower the correlation between trees?

Show the answer

If one feature is strongly predictive, only about 3 of every 10 splits can even see it. The first splits of different trees then differ, so their errors are less alike and the correlation between trees falls. With 100 trees and 3 of 10 features per split, only about 30 trees can use the strong feature at their first split. The other 70 must start elsewhere.

A common mix-up

The belief: "A deeper, more carefully fitted tree must beat an average of trees that each saw only part of the data." It is tempting because every individual forest tree is weaker than a tree trained on all rows and features. But the single tree's low training error is the problem: it has memorized noise. Each forest tree also memorizes noise, but different noise, and the average cancels much of it.

The math: variance of an average 8 min

Ten trees whose predictions each have variance 100 and pairwise correlation 0.3 give an average with variance 37, not the 10 that independence would give. The general formula behind these numbers follows. Let each of B trees give a prediction at a fixed point x. Treat the predictions as identically distributed random variables, each with variance σ² and with positive pairwise correlation ρ. Correlation here measures how similar two trees' predictions at x are, across random draws of the training data and of each tree's random choices (bootstrap and feature subsets). The variance of their average is, in the textbook treatment of random forests by Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (The Elements of Statistical Learning, chapter 15):

Var(average) = ρσ² + ((1 − ρ)/B) · σ²

The first term does not depend on B. The second shrinks as B grows. So adding trees removes the second term and leaves the first, which is the floor: the lowest variance any number of trees can reach, set by how correlated the trees are.

Derivation.

  • The sum of the B predictions has variance Bσ² + B(B − 1)ρσ²: B variance terms and B(B − 1) ordered pairs, each with covariance (the joint variability of two predictions, which equals ρσ² here).
  • Divide by B² to get the variance of the average: σ²/B + ((B − 1)/B)ρσ².
  • Substitute (B − 1)/B = 1 − 1/B: ρσ² + (σ²/B)(1 − ρ), which is the formula above.

[Bσ² + B(B − 1)ρσ²] / B² = ρσ² + ((1 − ρ)/B)σ²

Check with B = 3, σ² = 100 and ρ = 0.3. Directly: (300 + 6 × 30)/9 = 480/9 = 53.3. By the formula: 30 + (0.7/3) × 100 = 53.3. The two agree.

Two limits show the formula's range, with σ² = 100 and B = 10. If ρ = 0 (independent trees), the variance is 0 + (1/10) × 100 = 10, the plain 1/B gain from the second recall question. If ρ = 1 (identical trees), it is 100 + 0 = 100: no gain at all.

Numeric case. Take σ² = 100, so each tree has standard deviation 10, and ρ = 0.3.

  • B = 1: 0.3 × 100 + (0.7/1) × 100 = 30 + 70 = 100. One tree is the baseline.
  • B = 10: 30 + (0.7/10) × 100 = 30 + 7 = 37.
  • B = 100: 30 + (0.7/100) × 100 = 30 + 0.7 = 30.7.
  • B → ∞: the second term vanishes and the variance is 30.

Now raise the correlation to ρ = 0.8. With B = 100 the variance is 80 + (0.2/100) × 100 = 80.2. One hundred highly correlated trees barely beat one tree.

This is why the feature subset exists. Lowering the number of candidate features per split lowers ρ, and the floor falls with it. The textbook notes the cost: fewer candidates also raise each tree's bias (The Elements of Statistical Learning, chapter 15).

Check yourself

With σ² = 100 and ρ = 0.5, a forest has B = 50 trees. Which is its variance?

Show the answer

Variance = ρσ² + ((1 − ρ)/B) σ² = 50 + (0.5/50) × 100 = 50 + 1 = 51. Even 50 trees leave half the variance, because ρ is large.

Check yourself

With σ² = 100 and ρ = 0.3, how many trees keep the variance within 1 of the floor?

Show the answer

Require (0.7/B) × 100 ≤ 1, so B ≥ 70. At B = 70 the variance is 31. Many more trees than that buy almost nothing.

Check yourself

Single-tree variance is 100. A forest with B = 25 trees has variance 40. Find ρ.

Show the answer

40 = 100ρ + (1 − ρ)(100/25) = 96ρ + 4, so ρ = 36/96 = 0.375.

A common mix-up

The belief: "More trees always means a better forest, without limit." Adding trees never hurts the variance, but the improvement stops at ρσ². Past that point, only less correlated trees help. Also, adding trees does not cause overfitting in the way that deepening a single tree does.

What happens to bias 3 min

A forest of deep trees keeps the bias of one tree and cuts the variance, so the total error falls. The reason is that the expected value of an average is the average of the expected values. Averaging B trees trained the same way therefore leaves the bias of a typical tree unchanged, and only the variance falls. Wikipedia states the aim: averaging deep trees lowers variance, at the expense of a small increase in bias (Wikipedia: Random forest). The small increase arises because bootstrap samples and feature subsets make each tree slightly weaker than a tree grown on everything.

In bias-variance terms, expected squared error equals bias squared plus variance plus irreducible noise. Irreducible noise is the variation in the target that no model using these features can predict. A deep tree has near-zero bias and large variance. The forest keeps the near-zero bias and cuts the variance, so the sum falls. The noise term is untouched, and no ensemble removes it.

Numeric case.

  • Deep tree: bias² 4 + variance 100 + noise 25 = 129.
  • Forest (ρ = 0.3, B = 100): bias² 9 + variance 30.7 + noise 25 = 64.7.
  • The 5-point bias rise costs far less than the 69.3-point variance drop.
Check yourself

A forest raises bias² from 4 to 12 and cuts variance from 100 to 80, with noise 25. Is it better than the single tree?

Show the answer

The single tree scores 4 + 100 + 25 = 129. The forest scores 12 + 80 + 25 = 117. It is better, but only by 12: the 8-point bias rise eats 8 of the 20 points of variance gain.

Recent research reports that random forests can also reduce bias relative to plain bagging in some settings (Liu and Mazumder, arXiv preprint). For your exam, the standard account above is the one to state.

Seeing it in code 3 min

In scikit-learn, RandomForestRegressor defaults to max_features=1.0, which is plain bagging; setting max_features=0.33 adds the random feature subset (RandomForestRegressor documentation). Its prediction is the mean of the trees' predictions, and the code below checks that claim directly. The parameter n_estimators is the number of trees B.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_regression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error

X, y = make_regression(n_samples=500, n_features=10, noise=25, random_state=0)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)

tree = DecisionTreeRegressor(random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
forest = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=200, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
forest_sub = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=200, max_features=0.33,
                                   random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)

print(mean_squared_error(yte, tree.predict(Xte)))
print(mean_squared_error(yte, forest.predict(Xte)))
print(mean_squared_error(yte, forest_sub.predict(Xte)))

# The forest prediction is the mean of the individual tree predictions
per_tree = np.array([t.predict(Xte) for t in forest.estimators_])
print(np.allclose(per_tree.mean(axis=0), forest.predict(Xte)))  # True

Run it yourself. Both forests' test errors should be clearly below the single tree's, and the last line prints True.

Worked example and practice 3 min

Worked example

Single trees have variance 64 at a point. A forest of B = 16 trees has correlation ρ = 0.25. Find the forest's variance and the standard deviation of its prediction.

  1. Write the formula: ρσ² + ((1 − ρ)/B) σ².
  2. Compute the floor term: 0.25 × 64 = 16.
  3. Compute the shrinking term: (0.75/16) × 64 = 3.
  4. Add them: 16 + 3 = 19. The standard deviation is the square root of 19, about 4.4, against 8 for one tree.
Your turn

Single trees have variance 36. A forest has B = 9 trees and ρ = 0.5. Step 1 is given: the formula is ρσ² + ((1 − ρ)/B) σ². Compute the floor term, the shrinking term and the total. Then state the variance the forest would approach with unlimited trees.

Show a solution

Floor term: 0.5 × 36 = 18. Shrinking term: (0.5/9) × 36 = 2. Total: 20. With unlimited trees the variance approaches 18, so the nine trees already capture most of the possible gain.

More practice

A. Single trees have variance 64 and ρ = 0.25. How many trees keep the variance within 1 of the floor?

B. σ² = 100, ρ = 0.1, B = 100. Find the variance.

C. A deep tree has bias² 6, variance 81 and noise 16. A forest of the same trees has ρ = 0.2, B = 25 and bias² 10. Which has the lower expected squared error?

Show the answers

A. The floor is 16 and the shrinking term is 48/B. Requiring 48/B ≤ 1 gives B ≥ 48, where the variance is 17. B. 10 + (0.9/100) × 100 = 10.9; a lower ρ gives a much lower floor, as Figure 3 shows. C. The tree scores 6 + 81 + 16 = 103. The forest variance is 0.2 × 81 + (0.8/25) × 81 = 18.792, so it scores 10 + 18.792 + 16 = 44.8. The forest is better.

Your turn, no steps given

Single trees have variance 49, the correlation is ρ = 0.2 and B = 10. Write the formula, compute the total, and state the floor.

Show a solution

Floor: 0.2 × 49 = 9.8. Shrinking term: (0.8/10) × 49 = 3.92. Total: 13.72, against a floor of 9.8.

Summary 1 min

  • A deep tree has low bias and high variance; the forest averages many such trees to cut the variance.
  • Bootstrap samples and random feature subsets make the trees differ, so their errors partly cancel.
  • Variance of the average is ρσ² + ((1 − ρ)/B) σ²; more trees only remove the second term.
  • Bias stays about the same, or rises slightly, so the total error falls.
